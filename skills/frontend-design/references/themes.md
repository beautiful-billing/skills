# Themed, not costumed

Read this when the human asks for a site that looks like something: "make it look like a
newspaper", "like a spaceship", "like an old letter", "make it feel like Paris". Also read it
before you pick a design direction from a recipe.

The human is asking for a feeling, not a prop. They want visitors to sense the newspaper. They
don't want a fake newspaper. A good designer hears "newspaper" and gives them confident serif
headlines, a strict column grid and one ruled line. A bad one gives them blackletter, a weather
report, an issue number and "Price: one penny". The first looks like a professional's site with
a point of view. The second looks like a school play.

## Professional first, themed second

Build the site a top studio would build for this person, then let the theme season it. Nine
parts of ten are the same on every good site: clear navigation in plain words, a calm page, one
obvious next step, body text that's easy to read. The theme lives in the last tenth.

Spend the theme on three things, and only these:

1. **Type.** One display face that carries the mood, used for headings. Body text in a quiet,
   readable face. This alone says most of what the human asked for.
2. **Color.** A base that's nearly neutral (paper, white, near-black) and one or two accents
   from the theme's world. Not five.
3. **One motif.** A single structural idea from the theme, done well and used the same way
   everywhere: a ruled line, a column grid, a corner bracket, a card. One.

If the site still says "newspaper" with the motif removed, the type and color are doing their
job. If it only says "newspaper" because of the props, start again.

## Translate, don't reenact

| They ask for | Give them | Leave out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A newspaper | A sharp serif for headlines, a column grid, a hairline rule under the header, a dateline-style line for dates | Blackletter mastheads, weather, prices, issue numbers, "Late edition", fake ads |
| A spaceship, sci-fi | A geometric or technical sans, a dark calm base with one cool accent, thin lines and precise spacing | HUD brackets on every box, glowing text, scanlines, "All systems nominal", fake ship clocks |
| A letter, an invitation, Regency | An elegant serif, cream and one deep color, centered type with generous margins, one fine rule | Wax seals, torn or deckled edges, script fonts beyond the logo, "requests the pleasure" copy |
| A case file, a thriller | A strong condensed sans, high contrast, a tight grid, one red accent used sparingly | Stamps, paper clips, redaction bars, typewriter fonts for text, "Classified" everywhere |
| A children's book, playful | A friendly rounded face, a bright but limited palette (two colors plus neutral), big tap targets, the book's own art | Every button a different color, wobbly borders on everything, five handwritten fonts |
| A recipe box, a kitchen | A warm serif, a soft paper tone, one food color, a card used once for the recipe itself | Tape, gingham, coffee rings, typewriter body text, handwritten notes in every section |
| A city, a country ("Paris") | Type and color associated with the place (a Didone, a muted palette), real photos if they have them | Eiffel Tower clip art, flags, accordions, fake French in the labels |

## The rules

- **Navigation and buttons use plain words.** "Books", "About", "Events", "Newsletter". Not
  "The Desk", "Dossier", "Parish notices", "Deliver it to me". Visitors must never decode the
  site to use it. In-character copy is allowed once, in a small place (a tagline, a footer
  line), and only if it's genuinely good.
- **Novelty fonts are for the name, at most.** Blackletter, script, typewriter, stencil,
  "space" and handwriting faces can set the logo or wordmark. Never headings, body, navigation,
  labels or buttons.
- **No fake objects.** Stamps, tape, paper clips, wax seals, pins, torn edges, coffee stains,
  sticky notes, ribbons, file tabs. One, small, in one place, if the human asked for exactly
  that. Usually none.
- **No fake data.** Weather, issue numbers, coordinates, ship time, case numbers, "Status:
  nominal" readouts. They're noise. Real data (a release date, a page count, a series order)
  in a calm layout is better.
- **Textures are barely there or absent.** A paper tone is a color, not a pattern. If a visitor
  notices the texture, it's too strong.
- **Few all-caps labels.** Tracked capitals above every heading are the commonest tell of a
  costume. Use them for one kind of label, or none.
- **One accent, one radius, one shadow.** Every button the same shape and color. Primary in
  the accent, the rest quiet.
- **The content is the star.** On an author's site, the covers already carry the genre: a
  thriller cover looks like a thriller. The page frames them in calm space; it doesn't compete.
  The same goes for a photographer's photos, a chef's food, a product's screenshots.

## Check it before you show it

Look at a screenshot of the home page at desktop and phone width, and ask:

1. **The publisher test.** Would a big publisher, label or agency put this on their client's
   site? Would the human be proud to send it to their agent, their investors, a journalist?
2. **The squint test.** Squint at it. Do you see one clear hierarchy (name, the main thing, one
   button), or a lot of busy decoration?
3. **The removal test.** Take away the motif. Does it still feel like the theme? Then the type
   and color are right. Now take away one more decorative thing, because there's always one.
4. **The words test.** Could a visitor who skims find the books, the buy button and the
   newsletter in five seconds, without reading any themed copy?

If any answer is no, cut decoration until it's yes. Never add more to fix it.

## When the human pushes for more

Some people do want the full costume. Show them the restrained version first, as one of two or
three directions with `preview_options` (one of them a little bolder), and say why in one line:
"This keeps the newspaper feel through the type and the grid, so it reads as a real author's
site." If they still pick the bolder one, it's their site. Build it, and keep the rules above
that protect readers: plain navigation, readable body text, real buttons.
